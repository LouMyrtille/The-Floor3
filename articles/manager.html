Why Your Best Bartender May Be Your Worst Manager

When technical excellence becomes the wrong measure of leadership — and why hospitality needs another way to reward its best people.

Oh, my favourite subject again: managers. I did say I would revisit them.
Today, though, we are going to investigate the promotion system—the machine, so to speak, that produces them.
As we all know, a manager is not just a fancy bartender. They should possess skills that a bartender doesn't necessarily need. Yet every manager I know was once a bartender. In fact, they most likely became a manager precisely because they were a good bartender.
I have been wondering what made us see management as the natural progression of a bartending career. When the question who should get promoted? comes up, what qualities should we actually prioritise?
While trying to answer that question, I came across a study by Kay and Russette, Hospitality-Management Competencies: Identifying Managers' Essential Skills. The competencies considered essential across managerial positions included recognising guests' problems, maintaining professional and ethical standards, adapting creatively to change and cultivating a climate of trust.
I don't know about you, but whenever I assessed a bartender, these were never the focal points on the certification sheet. Technical standards—whether the ice was at the right level, whether a spec was followed correctly—were prioritised.
And very rightly so.
It was bartending I was assessing, not managerial ability.
Of course, some of the aforementioned competencies fall under the broader umbrella of customer service and are useful behind the bar. I am not suggesting that technical expertise is irrelevant. I am suggesting that it exists within a much broader managerial skillset.
I learned how to read people very well during my bartending years. It helps. It saves time. Knowing how to recommend the right drink to a guest can do wonders for your sales and tips. People skills are transferable.
But there is an enormous difference between managing a short interaction with a guest and overseeing, developing and retaining an entire team.
The study by Shum, Gatling and Shoemaker, A Model of Hospitality Leadership Competency for Frontline and Director-Level Managers, examines exactly that. The researchers surveyed 98 director-level hospitality managers about the competencies required at different levels of management. They grouped leadership competencies into three broad categories: business leadership, personal leadership and people leadership.
But here comes the interesting part—and perhaps their most important finding for this conversation: the closer a manager is to frontline employees, the more important their ability to manage people becomes.
Yet hospitality frequently promotes people because they demonstrate operational ability.
Operational competence is certainly relevant to management. But it is not synonymous with it.
I once worked with a very competent GM. He managed to double the revenue of his business in less than six months while maintaining a happy and productive team. Once, he said something that really stuck with me:
“The most important thing is that people try and cooperate well. I always thought that a group of people who know how to work together is more profitable than a bunch of experts who can't communicate.”
Many bartenders would find this counterintuitive. But this man prioritised team chemistry over individual technical excellence, and it paid off.
Essentially, being a manager means caring about the broader picture of the business while trusting and nurturing the people beneath you to take care of what you no longer have the time—or perhaps even the expertise—to do yourself.
A bartender can demonstrate enormous operational competence. Management asks a different question: can you manage yourself, your people and the business?
And this is where things become uncomfortable.
The study by Benson, Li and Shue, Promotions and the Peter Principle, found that top-performing salespeople were considerably more likely to be promoted. Yet pre-promotion sales performance was negatively associated with subsequent managerial performance.
In other words, being exceptional at the job that earns you a promotion does not necessarily mean you will be exceptional at the job you are promoted into.
Considering all this, one might wonder: why do companies continue promoting their highest performers if doing so can produce worse managers?
Because promotion is not only a method of filling vacancies. It is also a reward system.
Imagine being unquestionably the best employee in a company and watching somebody else get the promotion. You would be fucking furious.
And not without reason.
Companies need to reward technical excellence. The problem begins when management is the only meaningful reward available. Promotion then becomes compensation for being good at your current job rather than selection for the fundamentally different job that comes next.
So, who should get promoted?
My initial question remains unanswered, but I now think it was the wrong question all along.
Because the person who most deserves recognition and the person who would make the best manager are not necessarily the same person.
Which leads us to a much bigger hospitality problem: what career progression do we offer an exceptional bartender who shouldn't—or simply doesn't want to—manage people?
If the only way upwards is management, we force excellent bartenders to choose between stagnation and a job they may neither want nor be suited for. At the same time, we risk putting teams underneath managers selected primarily for skills that have very little to do with managing them.
Hospitality can be a rewarding career, but only if we make it one by putting the right people in the right roles.
Perhaps when deciding who should become a manager, we should pay more attention to their effect on people than to their technicality. Perhaps managing a business is not the natural development of every bartending career.
And if that is true, hospitality has another question to answer:
What do we offer our A-players if becoming a manager isn't the answer?
