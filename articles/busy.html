The Difference Between A Busy Bar And A Bar In The Shit

On teamwork, cognitive load and operational control

If you are a GM, I know you are already planning your Christmas outreach. How can you not? We are officially in the last days of summer. Before you know it, you'll already have tables booked for December and a massive forecast to reach. Not to mention the number of people you need to hire AND train before it's too late.
As we approach the busiest time of the year, memories emerge of chaotic shifts where being understaffed, irritated and often disorganised is almost the norm. I have begun asking myself what separates a busy bar from a bar “in the shit”.
So far, I have only worked in busy venues: large capacities, central streets, big revenues. If I have learned anything from researching operations, it is that a bar is a system. A factory, in a sense.
Let me clarify: a bar being busy doesn't necessarily mean that it's in the shit. Being busy simply means that demand is high. “In the shit”, though, signifies a failure of the system to keep up with that demand.
If you have been in hospitality long enough, you probably remember the time you worked with your “dream team”. I certainly do. It's nostalgic, isn't it? You all seemed to be perfectly synchronised, physically and mentally. Communication was clear and fast. Everything worked like clockwork.
This relates to what Eduardo Salas and other researchers in team cognition describe as shared mental models. Effective teams develop a common understanding of what is happening, what everyone is responsible for and what is likely to happen next. These kinds of teams are able to coordinate more effectively under high workloads.
Once, I worked a severely understaffed shift. It was only me and another bartender, who was luckily very skilled. The service ran without drama, like every other day. The standards were there. After closing, while we shared a couple of cold Coronas, he said:
“It's easy working together. We don't even need to talk.”
Sometimes it's not about what you say, but what you don't need to say. Perhaps good teams don't only communicate well. Perhaps the amount of communication they need is significantly lower because they already understand each other.
Being a good manager or bartender often means being both a mind reader and a fortune teller. I learned the hard way, while working as a food runner, that you should always operate one or two hours ahead. Weeds appeared out of nowhere. Do you know how stressful this is when you have no clean cutlery? (I discovered later that we always had spare cutlery in the dining room, which made things easier.)
Officially, this is called situational awareness, a framework developed by Mica Endsley. It separates awareness into three levels: perception, comprehension and projection.
In bar terms:
Perception: “There are twelve orders.”
Comprehension: “All the cocktails require crushed ice and we are nearly out.”
Projection: “We are going to run out of crushed ice in five minutes, which will stop production.”
This is not just reacting to what the bar needs, but anticipating what the bar will need. While weak systems fix problems after they appear, strong systems prevent problems from becoming problems in the first place.
I want you to remember a horrible shift you had. One where you started panicking, sweating and becoming clumsy because you simply didn't know what else to do with yourself.
There was a time when I was learning how to be a waitress in a particularly disorganised dining room. The person training me was so nervous that his hands were shaking. Eventually, he showered an already furious lady with a tray of five double gin and tonics. We had to clean up the broken glass and refund the furious lady while other tables waited to be served.
This is where cognitive load, most notably associated with the work of John Sweller, becomes relevant: essentially, the horrifying moment when everything seems to collapse.
We are humans. Our working memory is limited. When a bar gets busy, we don't simply perform more physical actions. We are also tracking more orders, modifications, guests, requests, timings and problems simultaneously.
Once cognitive demand exceeds someone's available capacity, performance deteriorates. Then you have a feedback loop: every mistake equals a remake. Every remake creates additional work. That additional work increases the cognitive load, which makes another mistake more likely.
The bar hasn't merely become busier. Being busy has started generating additional work.
A team in the shit is not necessarily a bad team. The truth is that the system can collapse in any bar, no matter how well organised it is. That's where team coordination and reflexivity come into play. Teams need to examine what they are doing and adapt their strategies when the existing system stops working.
Halloween, four years ago. I was a barback. Everything was going to shit.
Occasionally, someone would shout:
“Guys, we need to be faster.”
As you can imagine, this didn't change a thing.
The night changed for the better, though, when my GM took over. She asked the host to stop taking walk-ins, the floor tenders to run the glassware and me to move onto service.
Nothing about the team's technical ability changed. Nobody suddenly became a better bartender. Nobody learned how to move twice as fast.
The system simply reorganised itself around the bottleneck.
“In the shit” is not a measure of how much work there is. It describes a loss of operational control.
A bar isn't in the shit because there are twenty tickets on the rail. It is in the shit when the team can no longer anticipate what happens next, when cognitive load begins generating additional work and when the system fails to reorganise itself around its bottlenecks.
There needs to be a functional system, and since we operate within that system, anticipation on our part is essential. But this doesn't mean the system will never need to adapt. In my opinion, that is the most important part.
The answer isn't always to move faster. Sometimes we need to recognise what is failing, reorganise and adapt before asking the people inside the system to work harder.
Only then can we identify weaknesses and perfect what we do.
